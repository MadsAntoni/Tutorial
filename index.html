How to Taste Wine

A structured approach turns every glass into a learning experience. Use the Five S's, then explore the reference guides below.

The Five S's

Hold the glass at a 45° angle against a white background or a light source. Observe three things: colour, clarity, and viscosity.

Colour & Hue — The shade tells you the grape variety and how old the wine is. Red wines move from purple-violet when young toward brick and tawny with age. White wines deepen from pale straw to gold to amber as they age or are made from riper grapes.
Intensity — Pale wines often suggest cool climates, delicate grapes, or young vintages. Deep, concentrated colour usually signals warm climates, thick-skinned grapes, or extended maceration.
Clarity — Most wines should be clear and bright. Cloudiness in still wines can indicate a fault — though some natural wines are deliberately unfiltered.
Legs / Tears — Swirl the glass then watch the rivulets run down the side. Slow, thick tears suggest higher alcohol or residual sugar (more glycerol). Fast, thin tears suggest a lighter, lower-alcohol wine.

Rest the glass on a flat surface and rotate it in small circles, or cradle the stem and swirl in the air. Three to four rotations is enough.

Why it matters — Swirling increases the wine's surface area, allowing volatile aromatic compounds to evaporate. Without swirling you're catching only the lightest, most volatile molecules.
How much — Over-swirling (10+ rotations) can actually dissipate the most delicate aromatics. A few controlled rotations is ideal.
Tip for beginners — Keep the base of the glass on a table and rotate. It's easier to control and avoids spills.

Take two sniffs: first a quick, passive one before swirling to catch the top notes; then a deeper inhale after swirling to access the full aromatic range.

Primary aromas — Come directly from the grape. Fruit (apple, cherry, blackcurrant), floral (rose, violet, elderflower), and herbal (grass, mint, green pepper) notes all fall here.
Secondary aromas — Produced during fermentation by yeasts and bacteria. Bread dough, brioche, butter, yoghurt, and cheese notes are secondary. Common in Champagne and oaked Chardonnay.
Tertiary aromas (Bouquet) — Develop through aging in barrel or bottle. Vanilla, cedar, tobacco, leather, earth, mushroom, dried fruit, and petrol (classic in aged Riesling).
Off-aromas — Musty cardboard (cork taint), vinegar (volatile acidity), struck match (reduction), or barnyard (brett) can signal a fault. See the Faults section below.

Take a small sip and let it coat every part of your mouth for five to ten seconds before swallowing. Different areas of the palate register different sensations.

Sweetness — Perceived first at the tip of the tongue. Dry wines have no sweetness; off-dry wines have a subtle hint; sweet wines are obviously so.
Acidity — Makes your mouth water — you'll feel salivation on the sides and under the tongue. High acidity = crisp and fresh (Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc). Low acidity = soft and round, sometimes flat.
Tannin — The drying, gripping sensation on your gums and inner cheeks. Only in red wines and some orange whites. Soft in Pinot Noir and Gamay; grippy in Cabernet Sauvignon and Nebbiolo.
Body — The weight and texture of the wine. Think of it like milk: skimmed = light body (Pinot Grigio), semi-skimmed = medium (Merlot), full-fat = full body (oaked Chardonnay, Barossa Shiraz).
Alcohol — Creates warmth in the back of the throat. Low-alcohol wines (<12%) feel lighter and cooler; high-alcohol wines (>14%) feel heavier and warmer.
Flavour — Does the palate confirm or contradict the aromas? Note whether the flavours evolve or stay constant — evolution suggests complexity.

After swallowing (or spitting at a tasting), assess what remains: how long does the flavour linger, and does it remain pleasant?

Finish / Length — Count the seconds of flavour after swallowing. Short (<5 sec) = simple wine. Medium (5–15 sec) = good quality. Long (>15 sec) = fine wine territory. The longer and more pleasant the finish, the higher the quality.
Balance — No single element should dominate aggressively. Does the fruit, acidity, tannin, alcohol, and sweetness feel like they belong together? Imbalance — harsh tannin with no fruit, heat with no body — is a quality flaw.
Complexity — Do multiple layers of flavour appear and evolve over time? Does the wine change from first sip to finish? Complexity is a hallmark of quality.
Typicity — Does it taste and smell like the grape variety and region it claims to be? A Chablis should be flinty and lean; a Barossa Shiraz should be ripe and full. Wines that taste true to their identity score higher on typicity.
Reading the Glass — Colour
White Wine
Pale Straw Golden Deep Gold Amber
Pale straw → young, cool-climate, neutral grapes (Pinot Grigio, Muscadet). Deep gold → ripe grapes, oak ageing, or botrytis (oaked Chardonnay, Sauternes). Amber → very old wine, oxidative ageing, or orange wine.
Rosé Wine
Pale Salmon Copper Deep Pink Orange-Pink
Pale salmon → brief skin contact, cool pressing (Provence). Copper and deep pink → longer maceration or warmer climate (Tavel, Bandol). Orange-pink may indicate an older rosé or some orange winemaking influence.
Red Wine
Purple-Violet Ruby Garnet Brick-Tawny
Purple-violet → young wine with lots of anthocyanins (young Malbec, Syrah). Ruby → moderate age or cool climate (Pinot Noir). Garnet → some development (mid-age Bordeaux, Rioja Reserva). Brick-tawny rim → significant age or warm-climate oxidation (aged Barolo, Rioja Gran Reserva).
Understanding Structure
🍋 Acidity
Low — Soft, flatHigh — Crisp, tart

Makes your mouth water — literally triggers salivation on the sides of the tongue. Acidity is wine's backbone, preserving freshness and enabling ageing. A wine without enough acid feels flabby; too much can feel sharp.

High: Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo
Low: Viognier, Grenache, Marsanne

🍇 Tannin
Low — Silky, smoothHigh — Grippy, firm

Only in red wines (and some skin-contact whites). Comes from grape skins, seeds, stems, and oak barrels. Creates a drying sensation on your gums and inner cheeks — like over-steeped black tea. Tannin is a key preservative for ageing.

High: Cabernet Sauvignon, Nebbiolo, Tannat, Sagrantino
Low: Pinot Noir, Gamay, Barbera

🥛 Body
Light — DelicateFull — Rich, weighty

The weight and texture of the wine in your mouth. Imagine skimmed milk (light-bodied), semi-skimmed (medium), and full-fat cream (full-bodied). Body is primarily driven by alcohol, residual sugar, and extract.

Light: Pinot Grigio, Muscadet, Gamay, Vinho Verde
Full: Oaked Chardonnay, Barossa Shiraz, Zinfandel

🍯 Sweetness
Dry — No sweetnessSweet — Obvious sugar

Perceived first on the tip of the tongue. Most table wines are dry (under 4 g/L residual sugar). Off-dry wines have a subtle hint. Sweet wines — Sauternes, German Auslese, Port — have significant residual sugar.

Dry: Most Burgundy, Bordeaux, Rioja
Sweet: Sauternes, Tokaji, German TBA, Port, Moscato

🌡️ Alcohol
Low — <11.5%High — >14.5%

Creates warmth and a slight burning sensation in the back of the throat. Alcohol adds body and richness, but too much unbalanced by fruit and acidity feels 'hot'. Check the label — it's legally required.

Low: German Riesling Kabinett, Vinho Verde, Moscato d'Asti
High: Barossa Shiraz, Zinfandel, Châteauneuf-du-Pape

⏱️ Finish
Short — <5 secLong — >15 sec

How long the flavour lingers after you swallow. A hallmark of fine wine is a long, complex, evolving finish. The best wines continue to develop flavour for 30 seconds or more — sommeliers call this 'persistence' or 'length'.

Long: Grand Cru Burgundy, aged Barolo, Premier Cru Chablis, vintage Champagne
Short: Simple everyday wines

Recognising Wine Faults
🍄
Cork Taint (TCA)
Affects ~3–5% of cork-sealed bottles
"Musty cardboard, damp newspaper, wet dog"

Caused by trichloroanisole (TCA), a compound formed when natural cork reacts with certain chlorine-based compounds. It suppresses fruit aromas and leaves a mouldy, flat character even at very low concentrations.

→ Return the bottle. There is no fix once the wine is affected.

🍂
Oxidation
Common in old or poorly stored wine
"Sherry-like, nutty, stale, bruised apple, flat"

Caused by excessive oxygen exposure — through a failed cork, poor storage (heat, light, upright), or winemaking error. The wine loses freshness and fruit, taking on a flat, oxidised character. Note: intentional in Sherry, Madeira, and Vin Jaune.

→ Return or discard if unintentional. Acceptable or desirable in oxidative styles.

🍶
Volatile Acidity (VA)
Low levels common; high levels = fault
"Nail varnish, vinegar, acetic acid, acetone"

Caused by acetic acid bacteria producing acetic acid and ethyl acetate. Low VA adds complexity — some premium Italian reds carry it deliberately. High VA is aggressive and unpleasant, signalling poor hygiene or bacterial infection.

→ Low levels: enjoy. High levels: return or discard.

🐴
Brettanomyces (Brett)
Debated — complex at low levels
"Barnyard, horse sweat, leather, Band-Aid, wet hay"

Caused by Brettanomyces yeast, often in poorly sanitised cellars or barrels. At low levels some tasters find it adds earthy, savoury complexity — especially in Rhône reds and some Bordeaux. At high levels it overwhelms fruit entirely.

→ Subjective: low brett divides opinion. High brett = fault.

🥚
Reduction
Often resolves with aeration
"Struck match, rubber, garlic, cabbage, rotten eggs"

Caused by sulphur compounds (hydrogen sulphide, mercaptans) from insufficient oxygen during fermentation or winemaking. Unlike most faults, reduction is often temporary — it can blow off with vigorous swirling or decanting.

→ Swirl vigorously or decant. If it persists after 30 minutes of air, it's a winemaking flaw.

💧
Unexpected Refermentation
Rare in modern still wines
"Unexpected fizz or prickling in a still wine"

Caused by residual sugar refermented in the bottle by surviving yeasts, creating CO₂. In still wines this is always a fault. It can also make the wine slightly vinegar-sweet with off-flavours. Entirely normal and intended in Champagne, Prosecco, and Vinho Verde.

→ Return the bottle if it's a still wine. Fine in intended sparkling styles.

Ageing & Vessels

Where a wine rests — and for how long — shapes its character as profoundly as the vineyard itself.

Why Wines Age
🔗
Tannin Polymerisation

Short, harsh tannin molecules link together into longer chains. The result feels softer and silkier on the palate — the transformation from a grippy young Barolo to a velvet-textured aged one.

💨
Controlled Oxidation

A small, steady intake of oxygen — through barrel staves or a cork — drives flavour development. Too much = oxidised and flat. Too little = reduced and sulphurous. The vessel determines the rate.

🌿
Fruit Evolution

Bright primary fruit gradually transforms: fresh cherry becomes dried cherry, then leather and earth. In white wines, crisp apple becomes honeyed quince, then lanolin and petrol. Primary gives way to tertiary.

🧪
Ester & Compound Development

Hundreds of chemical reactions produce new flavour compounds over time. TDN creates the petrol note in aged Riesling. Esters form from acids and alcohols. Pyrazines soften. Each molecule has its own timeline.

🧂
Acid & Crystal Changes

Malic acid converts to softer lactic acid during malolactic fermentation. Tartaric acid precipitates as harmless tartrate crystals (the glassy deposits sometimes found in bottles or on corks).

Oak Barrels
🇫🇷
French Oak
Quercus petraea / Quercus robur
Tight grain Subtle Elegant

The finest-grained of the major oaks. Slow, gradual flavour extraction produces delicate vanilla, spice, cedar, and gentle toast without overwhelming the wine. Adds silky tannin and complexity.

Used in: Burgundy, Bordeaux, Champagne, top Rioja, world-class Chardonnay and Cabernet everywhere.

~€700–1,200 per new 225L barrique. Forests: Allier, Tronçais, Vosges, Nevers.

🇺🇸
American Oak
Quercus alba
Wide grain Bold Aromatic

Wider grain means faster, more pronounced flavour transfer. Higher levels of oak lactones produce vivid vanilla, coconut, dill, and cream-soda notes. Can dominate lighter wines if used heavily.

Used in: Traditional Rioja, Australian Shiraz, Bourbon barrels used in wine ageing, US Cabernet and Chardonnay.

~€200–400 per barrel — significantly cheaper than French oak.

🇭🇺
Hungarian Oak
Quercus petraea (Carpathian)
Medium grain Spicy Aromatic

Grain between French and American. Notably spicy and aromatic — contributes cinnamon, clove, and toasty notes. High extractive content can add structure and colour. Gaining popularity as a quality, affordable alternative.

Used in: Tokaji (traditional), some Rhône and Bordeaux châteaux, adventurous New World producers.

~€300–500 per barrel.

🇭🇷
Slavonian Oak (Botti)
Quercus robur
Large format Neutral Oxidative

Used in vast casks (botti) of 2,000–30,000L. At this scale, the oak-to-wine ratio is tiny, so almost no oak flavour transfers. The primary role is gentle, slow oxidation and micro-breathing — softening tannins without adding wood character.

Traditional in: Barolo, Barbaresco, Brunello di Montalcino, Amarone. Preserves the grape's pure character.

Very large investment, but vessels last decades with proper maintenance.

🔥 Toast Levels
Light toast — More wood tannins and grain flavour. Subtle vanillin, herbal notes. Better structural contribution than aromatic.
Medium toast — The most versatile. Caramel, spice, vanilla, toasted bread. Most widely used for red and white wines.
Heavy toast — Smoky, roasted espresso, dark chocolate, char. Can overwhelm delicate wines. Works for bold reds and some whisky-influenced styles.
🔄 New vs Used Oak
1st
New barrel — Full oak flavour and tannin extraction. Powerful vanilla, toast, and spice. Used for flagship wines. Can overwhelm lighter grapes.
2nd
Second fill — ~50% of new-oak flavour. Subtle wood influence with gentler extraction. Widely used as a balance between character and freshness.
3rd
Third fill — ~20–25% of new-oak flavour. Mainly oxygen permeability and micro-breathing. Common for wines where freshness is paramount.
4+
Neutral barrel — Essentially no oak flavour. Acts like a breathable container — gentle oxidation only. Traditional for Barolo, Brunello, white Burgundy.
Alternative Vessels
⚙️
Stainless Steel
Reductive

Hermetically sealed — zero oxygen exchange. Preserves every last molecule of primary fruit aroma, freshness, and varietal character. Temperature-controlled via a jacket system. The modern standard for aromatic whites and early-drinking reds.

Used for: Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Grigio, Muscadet, Vinho Verde, Champagne base wines.

🥚
Concrete (Egg & Tank)
Neutral

Slightly porous — allows a tiny, even rate of oxygen exchange without any oak flavour. Adds subtle texture and a distinctive mineral quality. The egg shape creates a natural convection current, keeping lees in suspension without stirring.

Used by: Burgundy, Loire, Rhône, California, Argentina — especially for Chardonnay, Chenin Blanc, and Cabernet Franc.

🏺
Amphora & Qvevri
Ancient Method

Clay or terracotta vessels, sealed with beeswax. The Georgian Qvevri is buried in the earth, which maintains a constant cool temperature. Slightly porous, neutral in flavour, with an earthy, textural character. Extended skin contact in amphora creates orange wines.

Traditional in: Georgia (Rkatsiteli, Mtsvane). Revived globally by natural wine producers in Italy, Spain, France, and California.

🌳
Acacia Wood
Light & Floral

A lighter, less extractive alternative to oak. Acacia (Robinia) contributes delicate floral and honey notes without the heavy vanilla and spice of oak. Particularly suited to aromatic grape varieties where wood character should be minimal.

Traditional in: Alsace for Gewurztraminer and Pinot Gris. Also used in Burgundy and Loire for delicate whites.

🌰
Chestnut
Rustic & Tannic

Coarser-grained than oak, with higher tannin levels from the wood itself. Gives a rustic, slightly bitter character. Largely replaced by oak and steel, but still used by traditionalists in parts of Tuscany (Vino Nobile), Portugal, and southern France.

Traditional in: Parts of Tuscany, Corsica, Portugal (Vinho Verde region), and some old-fashioned Languedoc producers.

🔶
Foudre (Large Oak)
Gentle Oxidation

Large-format French or Slavonian oak vats, typically 2,500–10,000L. The vast size means minimal oak flavour transfer per litre, while the wood still allows gentle oxidative development. The favoured vessel when a winemaker wants oxygen's softening effects without wood character.

Traditional in: Alsace, southern Rhône, Châteauneuf-du-Pape, Beaujolais (Moulin-à-Vent), and Germany's large Fuder casks.

Lees Ageing — Sur Lie
🍞 What Are Lees?

Lees are the sediment of dead yeast cells and grape solids that settle after fermentation. Heavy lees (gross lees — grape pulp, skins, seeds) are usually removed quickly by racking. Fine lees (yeast cells) are the beneficial material kept for sur lie ageing.

🥣 Bâtonnage — Lees Stirring

Stirring the fine lees back into suspension with a long stick (bâton) at regular intervals. Increases the rate of autolysis, adds more creaminess, body, and texture. Also helps protect the wine from oxidation by consuming dissolved oxygen. Used in white Burgundy and barrel-fermented whites.

⚗️ Autolysis — Yeast Breakdown

As yeast cells die and break down, they release mannoproteins, polysaccharides, and amino acids into the wine. This process — autolysis — adds creaminess, roundness, and a distinctive biscuit, brioche, and bread-dough quality. The longer the contact, the more pronounced the effect.

🗓️ Duration Matters

Muscadet sur lie: minimum 5 months on lees before March release. Champagne NV: minimum 15 months. Vintage Champagne: minimum 36 months. Prestige cuvées: often 5–10 years. A wine aged briefly on lees gains freshness; aged long, it gains profound yeasty complexity.

Drink Young
0–3 years from vintage Pinot Grigio, Sauvignon Blanc, Muscadet, Vinho Verde, Gamay Beaujolais, Prosecco, most Rosé. Designed for freshness and primary fruit — ageing only diminishes them. Drink within a year or two of purchase.
5–10 Years

10–20 Years
Long-term agers Premier Cru Burgundy, Barolo, Brunello, aged Rioja Gran Reserva, Bordeaux Crus Classés, Hunter Valley Semillon, Chenin Blanc (Savennières), Riesling Spätlese. Tannins soften; primary fruit transforms to complex tertiary notes.
20–50+ Years
Legendary agers Grand Cru Burgundy, top Bordeaux, Barolo Riserva from great vintages, Sauternes, Tokaji Eszencia, German TBA/Eiswein, Vintage Port, Madeira (arguably immortal). Cellar conditions — 12–14°C, dark, humid, horizontal — become critical at this scale.
🔍 Signs a Wine Is Ready vs Past Its Peak
✓ Ready to Drink
  • Brick or tawny rim on reds
  • Soft, integrated, silky tannins
  • Tertiary aromas (leather, earth, dried fruit) prominent
  • Sediment present — decant carefully
  • Colour: deep golden in whites; ruby-garnet in reds
✗ Past Its Peak
  • Brown or amber colour in reds
  • Flat, oxidised, sherry-like aroma
  • Loss of all fruit — just decay and earth
  • Watery, thin texture with no fruit support
  • Unpleasant, vinegar-like finish

Wine Faults

Knowing what a flawed wine smells and tastes like is as important as knowing what a great one does. Most faults are detectable within seconds of opening a bottle — and sending one back, or setting it aside, is not snobbery. It is knowledge.

How to use this guide: When you open a bottle and something seems off, work through these faults from the top. Cork taint and oxidation account for the majority of faulty bottles you will encounter. Trust your instincts — if a wine smells wrong, it probably is.
Cork Taint (TCA)
Also known as "corked"
Return it
Smells of: wet cardboard, damp cellar, musty newspaper, mouldy cork. Suppresses all fruit aroma — the wine smells flat and lifeless beneath the must.
Caused by: 2,4,6-Trichloroanisole (TCA), a compound formed when chlorine-based compounds interact with naturally occurring moulds in cork. Affects 1–3% of cork-sealed bottles.
What to do: Return it to the restaurant or retailer — a corked bottle is always replaceable. No remedy exists. Switching to screwcaps eliminates the fault entirely.
Oxidation
Also: maderised, flat, tired
Return it
Smells of: sherry (on a wine not meant to be sherry), bruised apple, vinegar, flat stewed fruit. Red wines turn brick-brown at the rim; whites go deep amber.
Caused by: excessive oxygen contact — a leaking cork, too-warm storage, or a bottle that has been open too long. Also caused by poor winemaking with insufficient sulphur dioxide.
What to do: Return it if newly purchased. An oxidised wine cannot be recovered. Prevent it at home by refrigerating open bottles and using a vacuum stopper or inert gas.
Volatile Acidity (VA)
Also: vinegar taint, acetification
Return it
Smells of: vinegar, nail polish remover (ethyl acetate), balsamic. Often prickles in the nose and throat. A small amount of VA is normal; at high levels it is a fault.
Caused by: acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter) converting ethanol to acetic acid, typically due to oxygen exposure during winemaking or storage. More common in wines low in sulphur dioxide.
What to do: Return it. High VA is a winemaking fault. Note: some winemakers intentionally produce wines with elevated VA as a stylistic choice — always consider the context.
Reduction
Also: sulphurous, reductive notes
Often fixable
Smells of: struck match, rubber, rotten egg, burnt garlic. The wine smells closed, sulphurous, or like a just-lit match. Intensity ranges from faint (often resolves) to extreme (fault).
Caused by: excess hydrogen sulphide or mercaptans formed when yeast is stressed during fermentation, or from excessive sulphur dioxide use. Common in screwcap wines due to reductive ageing.
What to do: Decant vigorously and let it breathe for 30–60 minutes. Swirl hard in the glass. Mild reduction often dissipates completely; severe cases do not. A copper coin stirred in the wine (an old trick) can help.
Brettanomyces (Brett)
Also: barnyard, "Brett character"
Subjective
Smells of: barnyard, horse sweat, leather, Band-Aid, smoked meat, cloves. At low levels some find it complex and earthy. At high levels it overwhelms all other character.
Caused by: Brettanomyces yeast, which thrives in poorly sanitised barrels and wineries. Historically common in Rhône Valley and some Bordeaux. Controversial: traditional winemakers tolerate or embrace low levels; modernists eliminate it completely.
What to do: At low levels — enjoy it if you do, set it aside if you don't. High Brett is a fault; return it. There is genuine debate among wine professionals about acceptable levels.
Heat Damage
Also: maderised, cooked, heat struck
Return it
Smells of: stewed jam, prune juice, raisins, flat cooked fruit. The wine tastes dull, jammy, and lifeless — as if the fresh fruit has been cooked away.
Caused by: exposure to heat above 25–30°C, often during shipping or storage. The cork may push slightly out of the bottle — a tell-tale sign. The fault is irreversible and does not improve with time.
What to do: Return it, especially if newly purchased. Check the cork — if it protrudes above the capsule, heat damage is the likely culprit. Store wines away from direct sunlight and radiators.
Excess Sulphur Dioxide (SO₂)
Also: sulphured, SO2 hit
Usually harmless
Smells of: burnt match, swimming pool, sharp chemical sting. More often felt than smelled — an irritating sensation at the back of the nose or throat when you first sniff.
Caused by: over-use of sulphur dioxide, the wine industry's most common preservative. Common in cheaper, mass-produced wines. Unlike cork taint, SO₂ dissipates with oxygen.
What to do: Swirl vigorously for a minute and leave the glass. In most cases the sulphur note blows off within 5–10 minutes. If it persists after 30 minutes, the level is excessive.
Refermentation in Bottle
Also: prickly, unexpected fizz
Usually harmless
Smells of: yeast, fresh bread, slight cidery quality. The wine has unexpected bubbles — a slightly prickly or fizzy sensation in a wine that should be still.
Caused by: a small amount of residual sugar remaining in a supposedly finished wine, which then re-ferments in bottle. More common in cheap wines bottled at lower alcohol. May also occur in Pétillant Naturel intentionally.
What to do: If minimal and the wine otherwise smells good — drink it. If the wine smells yeasty and tastes sweet-sour (malolactic bacteria at work), set it aside. Rarely worth returning unless severe.
Mouse Cage (Mousiness)
Also: mousy taint
Context-dependent
Smells of: mouse cage, popcorn, slightly rancid grain. Unusual: the taint is often not apparent on nosing — it emerges on the palate, as the saliva raises the pH and reveals the volatile compounds.
Caused by: certain lactic acid bacteria or Brettanomyces producing tetrahydropyridine compounds. More common in natural and low-sulphur wines. Considered a definite fault by most; some natural wine producers treat it as acceptable.
What to do: Set it aside or return it if newly purchased. Mousiness does not resolve with aeration. If tasting a wine and it seems fine on the nose but leaves an unpleasant cereal aftertaste — this is likely the culprit.

From Vine to Bottle

Understanding how wine is made transforms how you taste it. Every decision in the cellar — when to pick, how long to macerate, which vessel to use — leaves a fingerprint in the glass.

The Winemaking Journey
1
Harvest — Timing is Everything
The single most consequential decision in winemaking: when to pick. Early picking preserves acidity and freshness but risks underripe tannins and green flavours. Late picking maximises sugar and phenolic ripeness but risks overripe, jammy fruit and potential rot. In classic regions, a committee may set the date by law; in progressive wineries, winemakers taste daily and decide themselves. Harvest can be mechanical (efficient, 24-hour operation) or by hand (selective, preserves fruit integrity, essential for top estates).
Sugar = alcohol Acid = freshness Phenolic ripeness = smooth tannin
2
Sorting & Destemming
Harvested grapes arrive at the winery and are spread onto a sorting table — either by hand or by optical sorting machine. The goal is to remove leaves, unripe berries, damaged or rotten fruit, and any debris. Whole-bunch fermentation (retaining the stems) is an option for winemakers seeking additional tannin, structure, and spicy complexity — particularly in Burgundy and the Rhône. For most wines, stems are removed before the next step.
3
Crushing & Pressing — Red vs White Split
Here is where red and white winemaking fundamentally diverge. The skins of red grapes contain tannin and colour — so red wines need time with their skins. White grapes are pressed immediately to separate the juice from the skins before fermentation.
Red Wine
Grapes are crushed (or left whole) and transferred to a fermentation vessel — skins, seeds, and juice together. Fermentation happens in contact with the skins. Colour, tannin, and flavour compounds are extracted during this maceration period.
White Wine
Grapes are pressed gently and the clear juice (must) is separated from the skins immediately. Some producers use cold settling to clarify the juice before fermentation. "Orange wines" (skin-contact whites) break this rule intentionally — skins are left in contact for hours to weeks.
4
Alcoholic Fermentation
Yeast converts grape sugars into alcohol and CO₂. The winemaker chooses between commercial yeasts (reliable, predictable, consistent from year to year) or ambient "wild" yeasts from the vineyard and winery environment (less predictable, often more complex and terroir-expressive). Fermentation temperature matters: cool fermentation (8–15°C) for whites preserves delicate aromatics; warm fermentation (20–30°C) for reds extracts more colour and tannin. Fermentation typically lasts 5–14 days for reds, 2–4 weeks for whites.
Red: Maceration Techniques
Punching down the cap (pigeage), pumping over (remontage), or whole-bunch carbonic maceration (Beaujolais) — each extracts tannin and colour at a different rate. More extraction = more structure and ageability.
White: Temperature Control
Stainless steel tanks with temperature-controlled jackets keep fermentation cool and aromatic. Oak fermentation (Burgundy Chardonnay) adds texture and allows micro-oxygenation, but reduces the intense fruit aromas of cool-tank whites.
5
Malolactic Fermentation (MLF)
After alcoholic fermentation, a secondary bacterial conversion can occur — or be deliberately triggered. Lactic acid bacteria convert harsh malic acid (green apple) into softer lactic acid (milk), reducing overall acidity and adding a creamy, rounded texture. Almost all red wines undergo MLF — it softens tannins and stabilises the wine. For whites, it is a stylistic choice: full MLF gives a rich, buttery Chardonnay; no MLF gives a crisp, acidic Chablis or Sauvignon Blanc.
MLF on = creamy, round, lower acid MLF off = crisp, fresh, higher acid
6
Ageing — Vessel & Time
After fermentation and MLF, the wine moves into an ageing vessel. The choice of vessel profoundly shapes the final wine. This is covered in full in the Ageing & Vessels tab — but the key question is always: how much oxygen exposure does this wine need? French oak barriques (225L) give the most controlled micro-oxygenation and subtlest oak flavour. Stainless steel tanks are fully reductive. Concrete eggs and large neutral oak give intermediate results without adding oak flavour.
New oak = oak flavour + micro-oxygen Stainless = pure fruit Old oak = texture, no oak flavour
7
Blending
Most wines in the world are blends — either of different grape varieties (Cabernet + Merlot in Bordeaux), different vineyard plots (Grand Cru blended across several parcels), or different batches/vessels of the same grape. Blending is an art: winemakers taste dozens of components and assemble the final wine to achieve complexity, balance, and consistency. Single-varietal, single-vineyard wines ("monopole") are a minority. Even Champagne's non-vintage blends up to 40+ different reserve wines from multiple years.
8
Fining & Filtration
Before bottling, most wines are clarified. Fining uses a substance (egg whites, bentonite clay, isinglass from fish bladders) that attracts and precipitates proteins, tannins, or other unstable compounds. Filtration passes the wine through a membrane to remove micro-organisms. Both processes stabilise the wine and improve clarity, but may strip some texture and complexity. "Unfiltered" and "unfined" wines are increasingly common — particularly among natural producers — and often display more depth but also require careful handling.
Egg whites = vegan concern Bentonite = vegan-friendly fining Unfiltered = sediment possible
9
Bottling & Closure
The final step — and another significant decision. Cork (natural or composite) allows slow micro-oxygenation and is traditional for age-worthy wines. Screwcap (ROTE, Stelvin) provides a perfectly reductive seal with no TCA risk — preferred in New Zealand and Australia. Glass stoppers and synthetic corks sit between the two. The closure does not determine quality, but it influences how a wine evolves over time. A wine sealed under screwcap will age differently from the same wine under cork — typically more slowly and more reductively.
Cork = traditional + TCA risk Screwcap = no TCA, reductive ageing DIAM = technical cork, very reliable

Regions & Terroir

Wine is, more than anything else, a product of place. The combination of climate, soil, topography, and tradition — what the French call terroir — gives each region its character.

The Four Pillars of Terroir
☀️
Climate
Temperature, rainfall, and sunshine hours determine grape ripeness. Cool climates give higher acidity and lower alcohol; hot climates more sugar, alcohol, and ripe fruit.
🪨
Soil
Drainage, mineral composition, and heat retention shape the vine's stress and the wine's texture. Clay retains water; gravel drains fast; limestone reflects light and adds minerality.
⛰️
Topography
Altitude cools temperatures at a rate of ~0.6°C per 100m. Slope angle and aspect (facing south in the north, north in the south) control sun exposure and frost risk.
👐
Human Hand
Vine density, pruning, yield management, and winemaking traditions — these too are terroir. The same plot vinified differently produces different wines.
Old World Regions

Europe's wine regions have centuries of tradition behind their styles. Wines are typically named after the place, not the grape.

🇫🇷
Bordeaux
South-West France
Maritime Gravel / Clay
Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Cabernet Franc
The world's most famous red wine region, split by the Gironde estuary. The Left Bank (Médoc, Graves) is Cabernet-dominant — structured and age-worthy. The Right Bank (Pomerol, Saint-Émilion) favours Merlot — plush and approachable. Classified in 1855, many estates have produced the same style for generations.
🇫🇷
Burgundy (Bourgogne)
Eastern France
Continental Limestone / Clay
Pinot Noir, Chardonnay
The benchmark for Pinot Noir and Chardonnay, nowhere else uses just two grapes to produce such a wide range of quality. The Côte d'Or — just 60km long — contains Grand Cru, Premier Cru, Village, and Regional appellations. The same geology can produce wines worth €20 or €2,000 depending on the precise plot.
🇫🇷
Rhône Valley
South-East France
Continental / Med. Granite / Limestone
Syrah, Grenache, Viognier, Roussanne
Two very different zones: the Northern Rhône (Hermitage, Côte-Rôtie) produces single-variety Syrah of immense concentration on steep granite hillsides. The Southern Rhône (Châteauneuf-du-Pape) blends up to 18 varieties, dominated by Grenache, producing rich, herb-scented, warming reds.
🇫🇷
Champagne
North-East France
Cool Continental Chalk / Limestone
Chardonnay, Pinot Noir, Pinot Meunier
The northernmost limit of successful grape growing in France produces a base wine so searingly acidic it demands transformation. The traditional method — secondary fermentation in bottle — creates the world's most celebrated sparkling wine. Chalky soils retain moisture and give the wine its electrifying mineral tension.
🇮🇹
Tuscany
Central Italy
Mediterranean Galestro / Alberese
Sangiovese, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot
Home of Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino, and Vino Nobile di Montepulciano — all built on Sangiovese. High acidity and firm tannins make Tuscan reds natural food wines. The "Super Tuscans" of the 1970s (Sassicaia, Tignanello) broke appellation rules by using Cabernet and French oak, forcing Italy to rethink its classification system.
🇮🇹
Piedmont
North-West Italy
Continental Limestone / Clay / Sand
Nebbiolo, Barbera, Dolcetto, Moscato
Barolo and Barbaresco — both from Nebbiolo — are Italy's most age-worthy reds. Barolo is known as "the wine of kings and the king of wines." The grape is late-ripening with sky-high tannins and acidity in youth, transforming over decades into ethereal rose, tar, and truffle. Soil type (Serralunga clay vs Barolo sand) determines the house style — traditional or modern.
🇪🇸
Rioja
Northern Spain
Continental / Atlantic Clay / Limestone / Alluvial
Tempranillo, Garnacha, Mazuelo
Spain's most famous red region classifies wines by ageing time rather than vineyard quality: Joven (no oak), Crianza (1yr oak), Reserva (1yr oak + 1yr bottle), Gran Reserva (2yrs oak + 3yrs bottle). Tempranillo provides the backbone — strawberry, leather, dried herb. American oak historically gave Rioja its distinctive vanilla, dill, and coconut signature; French oak is increasingly used for modern styles.
🇵🇹
Douro Valley
North-East Portugal
Extreme Continental Schist
Touriga Nacional, Touriga Franca, Tinta Roriz
The dramatic, terraced hillsides of the Douro are the birthplace of Port wine — fortified with grape spirit to arrest fermentation, retaining natural grape sugar. Unfortified Douro table wines are increasingly impressive: deeply concentrated, floral, and with a distinctly mineral schist character. Summer temperatures can exceed 40°C in this rain-shadow valley.
New World Regions

The New World labels wines by grape variety. Freedom from tradition has enabled bold experimentation — and remarkable consistency.

🇺🇸
Napa Valley
California, USA
Mediterranean Volcanic / Alluvial
Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Merlot
California's most prestigious valley produces some of the world's most expensive Cabernet Sauvignons. Long, warm growing seasons with cool evening breezes from San Pablo Bay deliver ripe, concentrated fruit without excessive heat. The 16 sub-AVAs within Napa each have distinct characters — Stags Leap for elegance, Rutherford for dusty tannins, Howell Mountain for mountain intensity.
🇺🇸
Willamette Valley
Oregon, USA
Maritime / Continental Jory / Willakenzie
Pinot Noir, Pinot Gris, Chardonnay
Often called America's answer to Burgundy, Willamette Valley's cool, wet climate and volcanic Jory soils are ideal for Pinot Noir. The wines show red cherry, earth, and silky tannins — more restrained than California, closer in spirit to Côte de Nuits. Oregon pioneered Biodynamic farming in the US and is a leader in sustainable viticulture.
🇦🇺
Barossa Valley
South Australia
Warm Continental Sandy / Clay / Loam
Shiraz, Grenache, Mataro (Mourvèdre)
Home to some of the world's oldest Shiraz vines — "old vine" status from pre-phylloxera plantings dating to the 1840s. Barossa Shiraz is the archetype of warm-climate, full-bodied red: dark plum, licorice, dark chocolate, and sometimes game or meat. Penfolds Grange, made here, is Australia's most iconic wine. Altitude in the Eden Valley subregion provides cooler conditions for elegant styles.
🇦🇺
Margaret River
Western Australia
Maritime Gravel / Sandy Loam
Cabernet Sauvignon, Chardonnay, Semillon
A narrow strip of land surrounded by ocean on three sides gives Margaret River a strikingly Bordeaux-like climate — dry summers, mild winters. The gravelly soils and cool sea breezes produce some of Australia's most elegant Cabernets and Chardonnays: precise, structured, and long-lived. The Semillon–Sauvignon Blanc blend is a regional signature.
🇳🇿
Marlborough
South Island, New Zealand
Maritime Stony / Silty
Sauvignon Blanc, Pinot Noir, Pinot Gris
Marlborough Sauvignon Blanc redefined the grape's international identity in the 1980s. Intense UV light, cool nights, and free-draining stony soils produce wines of extraordinary aromatic intensity: passion fruit, fresh cut grass, gooseberry, and a distinctive "Marlborough marker" of fresh capsicum. Cloudy Bay's debut in 1985 launched New Zealand onto the world stage.
🇿🇦
Stellenbosch
Western Cape, South Africa
Mediterranean Granite / Sandstone
Cabernet Sauvignon, Pinotage, Chenin Blanc
The heart of South African fine wine, Stellenbosch sits in a beautiful mountain-framed valley 50km from Cape Town. Granite soils on mountain slopes produce structured, mineral Cabernet Sauvignon. Pinotage — South Africa's crossing of Pinot Noir and Cinsault, bred here in 1925 — is a point of national pride, ranging from earthy and smoky to plush and modern-styled.

Wine Styles

Beyond still table wine, three great traditions have evolved over centuries: sparkling wines, fortified wines, and dessert wines. Each uses a different technique to achieve a different purpose.

🥂
Sparkling Wines
Bubbles are produced by trapping CO₂ during a secondary fermentation — the method determines the bubble quality and the flavour profile.
Champagne
🇫🇷 Champagne, France
Traditional Method
Secondary fermentation happens in the bottle. After ageing on the spent yeast (lees) — minimum 15 months for NV, 3 years for vintage — the sediment is frozen and expelled (disgorgement). The result: fine, persistent bubbles, creamy texture, and complex yeast-derived flavours of brioche, toast, and almond alongside the fruit. Grape varieties: Chardonnay (Blanc de Blancs), Pinot Noir and Meunier (Blanc de Noirs), or blends.
Key terms: Non-Vintage (NV), Brut Nature (bone dry), Extra Brut, Brut, Extra Dry, Demi-Sec
Cava
🇪🇸 Catalonia, Spain
Traditional Method
Produced by the same Traditional Method as Champagne but using native Spanish grapes: Macabeo, Xarel-lo, and Parellada. Cava tends to be earthier, with appley and citrus fruit, and a slight rustic minerality. Gran Reserva Cava (30+ months on lees) offers real complexity at a fraction of Champagne's price. Penedès near Barcelona is the epicentre.
Cava de Paraje Calificado = single-vineyard, highest quality tier
Prosecco
🇮🇹 Veneto & Friuli, Italy
Tank Method (Charmat)
The secondary fermentation takes place in a large pressurised tank, not bottle. This is faster, cheaper, and — crucially — preserves the primary fruit aromas of the Glera grape. Prosecco is meant to be drunk young and fresh: green apple, pear, white peach, and light floral notes. The bubbles are softer and larger than Champagne. DOCG zones Conegliano-Valdobbiadene produce the finest examples.
Cartizze = small, prestigious DOCG sub-zone; Rive = single-village wines
Crémant
🇫🇷 Multiple regions, France
Traditional Method
Traditional Method sparkling wines from regions outside Champagne: Crémant d'Alsace (Pinot Blanc, Auxerrois), Crémant de Loire (Chenin Blanc, Cabernet Franc), Crémant de Bourgogne (Chardonnay, Pinot Noir), Crémant de Limoux (Mauzac, oldest sparkling wine tradition in France). All offer genuine complexity at accessible prices, often aged longer than the minimum.
Look for vintaged Crémant from specific producers for the best quality
Pétillant Naturel
Worldwide
Ancestral Method
The oldest method: the wine is bottled before fermentation is complete, trapping natural CO₂ with no added dosage or sulphur. The result is gently fizzy, slightly cloudy, and often funky — sometimes with a few grams of residual sweetness. "Pét-nat" has become the calling card of natural wine producers worldwide. Unpredictable, lively, and polarising — nothing else quite like it.
No disgorgement — sediment stays in the bottle; can be cloudy and rustic
Lambrusco
🇮🇹 Emilia-Romagna, Italy
Tank Method (Charmat)
One of the world's few sparkling red wines — and the most widely exported Italian wine by volume. Made from the Lambrusco family of grapes (Lambrusco Grasparossa, Sorbara, Salamino) in the plains around Modena and Reggio Emilia. Most commercial Lambrusco is lightly sweet (amabile) with purple-red foam, a deep purple hue, and flavours of sour cherry, blackberry, and a characteristic tart freshness. The finest examples — from Lambrusco di Sorbara DOC — are bone-dry (secco), pale-ruby, almost rosato in colour, delicate and electric with acidity. Like Prosecco, it is produced in a pressurised tank to preserve fresh fruit character, though a small number of artisan producers use the Traditional Method.
Secco Lambrusco di Sorbara is one of Italy's great food wines — perfect with charcuterie, Parmigiano, and cured meats
Franciacorta
🇮🇹 Lombardy, Italy
Traditional Method
Italy's answer to Champagne, and arguably the country's most serious sparkling wine. Produced in the hills south of Lake Iseo from Chardonnay, Pinot Nero, and Pinot Bianco, Franciacorta DOCG requires secondary fermentation in bottle and a minimum 18 months lees ageing for non-vintage (30 months for vintage, 60 months for Riserva). The result is genuinely Champagne-like: fine persistent bubbles, toasty brioche character, and real depth. It remains less known internationally than its quality warrants — which means good value.
Franciacorta is the only Italian DOCG where the production method (Traditional Method) is part of the appellation rules
Moscato d'Asti
🇮🇹 Piedmont, Italy
Low-alcohol frizzante (Charmat variant)
Not quite still, not quite sparkling — Moscato d'Asti is frizzante (gently fizzy), typically just 5–5.5% alcohol, and always off-dry to sweet. Made from Muscat Blanc à Petits Grains in the hills of Asti, the wine ferments in a sealed tank and is bottled before fermentation fully completes. The result is light, delicate, and intensely aromatic: peach blossom, apricot, orange zest, honey, and fresh grape. Served ice-cold, it is one of the world's most seductive light wines and a perfect partner for fruit tarts, almond cake, or as an aperitif.
Not to be confused with Asti Spumante, which is fully sparkling and less refined
🍷
Fortified Wines
Grape spirit (brandy) is added during or after fermentation to raise alcohol to 15–22% — originally to preserve wine for long sea voyages.
Port
🇵🇹 Douro Valley, Portugal
Fortified during fermentation
Spirit is added mid-fermentation, killing the yeast and retaining natural grape sugar — hence Port is always sweet. The styles are wide: Ruby Port (young, fruit-forward, cheap); Tawny Port (aged in small casks, oxidative, nutty, dried fruit); LBV — Late Bottled Vintage (single year, aged 4–6 years); Vintage Port (from the finest years only, aged 20+ years in bottle, the pinnacle of the category).
Touriga Nacional gives Port its structure, violet aroma, and age-worthiness
Sherry
🇪🇸 Jerez, Spain
Fortified after fermentation
Fermented completely dry, then fortified — creating a base wine that is aged in the solera system (a fractional blending across multiple vintages). Fino and Manzanilla are bone-dry, delicate, and protected by a layer of flor yeast. Amontillado and Palo Cortado are aged first under flor, then exposed to air — developing nutty, amber complexity. Oloroso is fully oxidised: walnut, tobacco, dark fruit. Pedro Ximénez (PX) is intensely sweet from sun-dried Palomino grapes.
The most misunderstood wine in the world — Fino Sherry is one of the finest food wines
Madeira
🇵🇹 Madeira Island, Portugal
Fortified + heated (estufagem)
Perhaps the world's most indestructible wine. Madeira is deliberately oxidised and heated — either by passing through warm tanks (estufagem) or slow ageing in hot lofts (canteiro, the finest method). This produces a wine of astonishing longevity: bottles from the 1800s are still drinkable. Styles range from dry (Sercial) to rich (Malmsey/Malvasia). Notes of dried fruit, coffee, salted caramel, and a distinctive rancio quality.
Once opened, a bottle of Madeira keeps for months — it is already oxidised
🍯
Dessert & Sweet Wines
Natural sugar is concentrated — by botrytis, freezing, or drying — to produce wines of extraordinary richness and complexity.
Sauternes
🇫🇷 Bordeaux, France
Noble Rot (Botrytis)
In autumn, the Ciron river creates misty mornings and warm afternoons — the perfect conditions for Botrytis cinerea, "noble rot." The fungus pierces grape skins, allowing water to evaporate and sugar to concentrate. Grapes are harvested in multiple passes (tries), selecting only botrytised berries. The result: golden, honeyed wines with apricot, orange peel, ginger, and crème brûlée — balanced by Sémillon's waxy acidity. Château d'Yquem is the only Premier Grand Cru Supérieur.
A glass of Sauternes with foie gras or Roquefort is one of the world's great pairings
Eiswein / Ice Wine
🇩🇪 Germany / 🇨🇦 Canada
Frozen grapes
Grapes are left on the vine until temperatures fall below −8°C, then pressed while frozen. Because water freezes first, only intensely concentrated sugary juice is extracted. German Eiswein is made from Riesling or occasionally other varieties in exceptional years — delicate and needle-sharp with acidity. Canadian Icewine (often Vidal) is a more reliable product due to reliably cold winters, and is richer and more tropical in character.
Production is tiny — grapes must be hand-picked before dawn in sub-zero temperatures
Amarone della Valpolicella
🇮🇹 Veneto, Italy
Appassimento (grape drying)
Corvina, Rondinella, and Molinara grapes are dried on bamboo racks (arele) for 90–120 days, losing up to 40% of their weight. The concentrated juice ferments fully dry, producing a wine of 15–17% alcohol with an extraordinary density of dried cherry, chocolate, fig, spice, and bitter finish. The drying is done in well-ventilated fruttai lofts. Recioto della Valpolicella is the sweet version — fermentation arrested before dryness.
Ripasso wines are made by re-fermenting Valpolicella on Amarone grape skins — a "halfway house"
Vin Santo
🇮🇹 Tuscany, Italy
Appassimento + barrel oxidation
Trebbiano and Malvasia grapes are dried on mats or hung from rafters until Christmas, then pressed and sealed into small barrels (caratelli) for 3–10 years. The wine ferments and oxidises slowly, developing flavours of walnut, dried orange, honey, hazelnut, and toffee. The "holy wine" of Tuscany is traditionally served with almond biscotti (cantucci), which are dipped into the wine. Colour ranges from amber to deep mahogany.
The "Occhio di Pernice" style uses Sangiovese for a darker, even more complex version

Reading a Wine Label

A wine label tells you everything — if you know what to look for. The key divide is between Old World (place-centric) and New World (grape-centric) labelling conventions.

Old World vs New World
Old World Label
Place first. The grape is implied by tradition.
1
Producer / Estate
The family or château name. May or may not be prominent — some Old World labels are dominated by the appellation instead.
2
Appellation
The geographic origin — and the most important information on the label. "Pommard" tells you it's Pinot Noir from Burgundy. "Barolo" tells you it's Nebbiolo from Piedmont. You must know the regional conventions.
3
Classification / Quality Level
Grand Cru, Premier Cru, Reserva, DOCG — signals where in the regional quality hierarchy this wine sits.
4
Vintage
The year the grapes were harvested. In Old World regions, this matters greatly — weather varies significantly between years.
Example — Old World
Domaine de la Romanée-Conti
Échézeaux Grand Cru
Appellation Échézeaux Contrôlée · 2019
New World Label
Grape variety first. The place adds context.
1
Producer Brand
Often prominent. The winery name is the primary identity — many consumers buy by brand rather than region.
2
Grape Variety
Stated explicitly: "Cabernet Sauvignon," "Sauvignon Blanc." This is the defining information — no regional knowledge required to understand what the wine is.
3
Region / AVA / GI
More specific origin gives context: "Napa Valley" vs "California." The more specific, the stricter the grape and yield requirements.
4
Reserve / Special Tier
Terms like "Reserve," "Estate," "Single Vineyard" signal higher quality — but are largely unregulated in many New World countries. Evaluate on price and reputation rather than label claim alone.
Example — New World
Opus One
Cabernet Sauvignon
Napa Valley · 2019

The Science of Pairing

Great pairing is not magic — it is chemistry. Understanding why certain combinations work (and others clash) lets you build pairings with confidence, not just memorise rules.

Core Principles
💪
Tannins Need Fat or Protein
Tannins are polyphenols that bond with salivary proteins, creating a drying, astringent sensation. When fat or protein is present in food, the tannins bind to those molecules instead, softening dramatically and releasing flavour. This is why lean, tannic red wine tastes harsh and metallic with fish — there is no fat to neutralise the tannin. A Barolo with a rich Florentine steak, though? The tannins collapse into silk.
Try: Barolo + T-bone steak · Avoid: Cabernet Sauvignon + sea bass
⚡
Acidity Cuts Through Richness
High-acid wines act like a squeeze of lemon — they stimulate salivation, cleanse the palate, and reset it for the next bite. A rich, fatty dish (lobster with butter, pasta carbonara, fried food) paired with a low-acid wine feels heavy and monotonous after the first few bites. High-acid Chablis with oysters or creamy pasta is refreshing precisely because the acid cuts through the fat and restores freshness.
Try: Riesling + pork belly · Chablis + oysters · Champagne + fried chicken
⚖️
Match Weight to Weight
A powerful, full-bodied wine overwhelms a delicate dish, and vice versa. The body of a wine (light, medium, full) should mirror the richness and intensity of the food. A light Burgundy Pinot Noir alongside a simple roast chicken allows both to express themselves. The same chicken alongside Amarone disappears under the wine's intensity — you taste only the wine.
Light dishes → Pinot Noir, Pinot Grigio · Rich dishes → Barossa Shiraz, Barolo
🍰
Wine Must Be Sweeter Than the Dish
If a dish is sweeter than the wine, the wine tastes dry, thin, and acidic by comparison — even if it is technically off-dry. This is why dry table wine with dessert usually fails. The wine needs at least as much sweetness as the food, and ideally more. A bone-dry Brut Champagne served with a cream cake will taste harsh and sour. Demi-Sec Champagne or a Sauternes will harmonise beautifully with the same dish.
Try: Sauternes + crème brûlée · Avoid: Brut Champagne + chocolate mousse
🧂
Salt Softens Tannin & Bitterness
Salt suppresses bitterness perception — this is basic taste science, the same reason salt balances coffee. In wine pairing, this means salty foods make tannic wines taste softer and more approachable. Salty cheeses like Roquefort or Parmesan pair beautifully with Cabernet or Barolo. It also explains why Fino Sherry — high-acid, bone-dry — works brilliantly with salty cured ham (jamón ibérico).
Try: Barolo + aged Parmesan · Fino Sherry + jamón ibérico
🍄
Umami Amplifies Bitterness
Umami (glutamate) in food amplifies the bitter and astringent qualities in wine. This is why highly umami-rich foods — anchovies, sun-dried tomatoes, mushrooms, aged cheeses — can make tannic reds taste even more bitter. The solution is either to choose a lower-tannin wine, a richer wine where the tannins are already resolved by oak ageing, or to add fat and salt to the dish to counterbalance.
Richly umami dishes: use aged wines with softer tannins (e.g. Reserva Rioja)
🌍
Local Food, Local Wine
Centuries of co-evolution mean that the regional food and wine of any given place have grown up together — each shaping the other. Chianti's high acidity was shaped to cut through the olive oil and tomato in Tuscan cuisine. Alsatian Riesling's off-dry style balances the richness of choucroute and pork. Manzanilla Sherry's salinity echoes the ocean air of Sanlúcar. When in doubt, this principle alone will rarely steer you wrong.
Riesling + Alsatian tarte flambée · Chianti + pasta al ragù · Txakoli + grilled fish
🪞
Mirror or Contrast
There are two valid pairing strategies. Mirror: match like with like — rich Chardonnay with creamy sauce, earthy Pinot with earthy mushrooms, sweet Sauternes with sweet custard. Contrast: use opposites to create tension — crisp, high-acid wine against rich food, or sweet wine with salty cheese. Both work. The failure state is ignoring both strategies — choosing a wine at random without considering the dominant qualities of the food.
Mirror: Viognier + apricot chicken · Contrast: Sauternes + Roquefort
At a Glance: Harmony vs Clash
Classic Harmonies
  • Champagne + oysters (acid + salinity + umami)
  • Sauternes + foie gras (sweetness mirrors richness)
  • Barolo + truffle pasta (tannin + fat + earthiness)
  • Riesling + spicy Thai (acidity + sweetness cools heat)
  • Chablis + shellfish (mineral acidity mirrors the sea)
  • Fino Sherry + Iberian ham (salinity + oxidative nuttiness)
  • Pinot Noir + salmon (lighter tannins + omega-3 fat)
Common Clashes
  • Tannic red + oily fish (metallic, bitter result)
  • Dry red + sweet dessert (wine tastes acidic & harsh)
  • Heavy Shiraz + delicate sole (wine overwhelms food)
  • Oaky Chardonnay + artichoke (tannin from oak + cynarin = bitter)
  • Brut Champagne + chocolate (acidity vs sweetness)
  • High-alcohol red + very spicy food (alcohol amplifies heat)
  • Sweet wine + sour dish (clashing flavour profiles)

Serving & Glassware

How you serve a wine can be as important as which wine you choose. Temperature affects aroma and structure; glass shape directs wine to the right part of your palate; decanting transforms young tannic reds — or rescues old ones.

Serving Temperatures

Most wines are served too warm (reds) or too cold (whites). Even 2–3°C makes a significant difference to how a wine smells, tastes, and feels.

Style Temperature Why
Sparkling & Champagne 6–8°C Coldest. Preserves bubbles, keeps acidity crisp. Too warm = flat and flabby.
Sweet & Dessert Whites 8–10°C Cold highlights freshness and keeps sweetness from feeling heavy or cloying.
Light, Crisp Whites 8–10°C Pinot Grigio, Albariño, Muscadet — delicate aromatics at their best chilled.
Full-bodied, Oaked Whites 12–14°C Burgundy Chardonnay, oaked Rioja Blanco — complexity unfolds with slight warmth.
Rosé 10–12°C Provence rosé at 10°C is crisp and refreshing; slightly warmer to reveal fruit.
Light Reds 12–14°C Beaujolais, light Pinot Noir — even 30 min in the fridge opens them up beautifully.
Medium Reds 15–17°C Rioja Crianza, Côtes du Rhône — fruit shines without alcohol feeling harsh.
Full-bodied, Tannic Reds 17–19°C Barolo, Napa Cab — warmer allows tannins and aromatics to open. Over 20°C tastes flat and alcoholic.
Vintage Port & Fortified 16–18°C Warm enough to reveal complexity; too cold suppresses the dried fruit and spice.
Glass Shape

Glass shape is not marketing. The rim diameter, bowl depth, and taper direct wine to specific taste zones and concentrate or disperse aromas. The right glass makes a measurable difference.

Bordeaux Glass
Tall, straight-sided bowl. Directs wine to the back of the palate for a long finish and allows tannins time to resolve before hitting taste buds.
Cabernet, Merlot, Syrah, Malbec
Burgundy Glass
Wide, balloon bowl maximises surface area. Concentrates delicate Pinot Noir aromatics while directing wine to the tip of the tongue where sweetness is perceived first.
Pinot Noir, Nebbiolo, aged reds
White Wine Glass
Smaller, slightly narrowed bowl preserves cool temperature and focuses delicate fruit and floral aromatics. Less exposure to air than a red wine glass.
Chardonnay, Riesling, Sauvignon Blanc
Champagne Flute
Tall, narrow shape slows the release of bubbles and preserves effervescence. However, the coupe (flat-bowled alternative) allows better aroma development at the cost of faster bubble loss.
Champagne, Cava, Crémant
Sherry Copita
Small, tulip-shaped copita traps the complex oxidative aromas of Sherry or fortified wines. The taper concentrates the nutty, saline aromas and prevents them dissipating into the room.
Sherry, Madeira, old Tawny Port
Decanting

Decanting serves two completely different purposes depending on the wine's age. The technique differs accordingly.

Young Tannic Reds
Purpose: Aeration — open it up
Young Barolo, Napa Cabernet, Hermitage, or northern Rhône Syrah can be harsh, closed, and reductive when first opened. Decanting exposes the wine to oxygen, allowing volatile sulphur compounds to dissipate and encouraging tannins to begin softening. Pour slowly and deliberately into a wide-bellied decanter. Ideal time: 1–4 hours before serving. The more tannic and younger, the longer the decant. For very robust young wines, you can decant and then pour back into the bottle (double decanting).
Old, Aged Reds
Purpose: Separation — remove sediment
Wines aged 10+ years in bottle develop sediment from the precipitation of tannins and tartrate crystals. This is natural and harmless but gritty and unpleasant. Stand the bottle upright for 24 hours to allow sediment to fall to the bottom. Decant slowly and steadily, with a light or candle behind the bottle neck — stop pouring the moment you see sediment approaching the neck. Old wines are fragile: once decanted, serve within 30–60 minutes before oxygen degrades the wine.

Vintages

The year on a bottle tells you when the grapes were harvested — and in regions where weather varies significantly between years, it can be the single most important piece of information on the label.

Why the Year Matters
🌦️
Growing Season Weather
The entire growing cycle — from bud break in spring to harvest in autumn — is shaped by rainfall, temperature, sunshine hours, and timing. A warm, dry summer builds ripeness. Rain at harvest dilutes flavour and risks rot. Frost in spring destroys young shoots. No two years are alike.
🍇
Ripeness & Phenolics
A warm year ripens tannins fully (ripe, round) and boosts sugar (higher alcohol). A cool year gives higher acidity, lower alcohol, and sometimes underripe green tannins. Neither is automatically "better" — context determines which is more desirable.
🗺️
Regional Variation
The same year that was catastrophic in one region may have been outstanding 200km away. Burgundy 2012 was difficult (frost, hail); Barolo 2012 was exceptional. Always check vintage assessments region by region, not as a global judgement.
🏖️
New World Consistency
New World regions (Napa, Barossa, Marlborough) have more reliable, predictable climates — fewer vintage disasters. Old World regions (Burgundy, Bordeaux, Germany, northern Italy) are more variable, which is why vintage knowledge matters far more when buying European wine.

How to Use Vintage Information
💡
Buy Smart
In great vintage years, even lower-tier wines benefit from excellent raw material. In challenging years, only the best producers have the skill and resources to produce good wine — so buy at the top end or look to other regions.
⏳
Age-Worthiness
Great vintages tend to produce wines with more tannin and acidity — the preservatives that allow long ageing. An exceptional Bordeaux from 2015 or 2016 should peak in 20+ years. An average vintage's wines are typically for earlier drinking.
🔄
Challenging Isn't Worthless
Difficult years often produce elegant, lighter-styled wines that can be more approachable young. And they are always cheaper at auction. A 2013 Burgundy — a generally difficult year — from a skilled producer can be beautiful precisely because lower ripeness produced fine, precise wines.
🍾
Non-Vintage Wines
Champagne NV, Sherry, Tawny Port, and many other wines carry no vintage on the label because they blend multiple years. The solera or blending process is designed to create consistency across vintages — the winemaker's skill, not the year's weather, determines the quality.
